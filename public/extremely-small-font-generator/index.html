How to use the extremely small font generator

  1. Type or paste your text into the box above.
  2. Start with superscript — it is the smallest and has the most complete alphabet.
  3. Fall back to small caps if superscript is too small to read in your font, or if the text needs to carry real meaning.
  4. Click “Copy” and paste. The characters are plain text and need no formatting support at the other end.

Extremely small font generator FAQ

Which is actually the smallest font?

Superscript. Its characters are drawn at roughly half the height of a normal letter, against about 70% for small caps and a similar size for subscript. Superscript is also the only one of the three with a complete lowercase alphabet, so a whole phrase stays legible. If you want the smallest text that is still readable at a glance, superscript is the answer.

Can I make text smaller than this?

Not with characters. There is no Unicode block smaller than the superscript and subscript sets, and no way to set a font size in a plain-text field — that is formatting, and formatting is exactly what these fields strip. Anything claiming smaller text is either using a real font on a page it controls, or an image.

Why do some letters stay full size?

Coverage is uneven. Superscript has every lowercase letter and all ten digits, but capital Q is missing. Small caps covers a–z except X, which keeps its normal form because a lowercase x is already the right shape. Subscript is the thinnest: nine letters have no small form at all. The generator leaves those alone instead of substituting something misleading.

Does tiny text work in an Instagram bio?

Yes. Bios, captions, comments and display names on Instagram, TikTok, X/Twitter, Facebook, Discord, Steam and most games all accept these characters, because they are ordinary text. The usual exception is the @handle, which many platforms restrict to plain ASCII.

Is extremely small text readable on a phone?

A few words, yes. A full sentence in superscript is genuinely hard to read on a small screen, and some people will not be able to read it at all. Use it for one line, a sign-off or a quiet aside — not for anything that carries real information.

Is it bad for accessibility?

It is worth knowing the cost. Screen readers handle these characters inconsistently: some announce them by their phonetic names, some skip them. Anyone relying on one may get nothing from a bio written entirely in tiny text, so keep the important parts in normal letters.
